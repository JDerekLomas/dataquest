The Broad Street Pump

In the late summer of 1854, a cholera outbreak killed hundreds of people in a few Soho streets in just days. The cause was a mystery — most doctors blamed "bad air." A physician named John Snow had a different idea, and he tested it with a map. In this lesson you'll retrace his investigation with his real data — 578 deaths and 13 water pumps — and discover how a picture of points became proof.

Step 6 of 9

Add the suspect: water pumps

Soho got its drinking water from public street pumps. Snow's hunch was that cholera spread through water, not air — so he added all 13 neighborhood pumps to the map.

Press Nearest pump to color each death by the pump closest to it. One pump's color floods the cluster: 359 of the 578 deaths are nearest to it.

Step 7 of 9

The distribution lens

Leave the map for a moment. Count how many deaths are nearest to each pump and stack the counts into a bar chart. Grouping data by a category — here, the nearest pump — is one of the most powerful moves in analysis.

The shape is lopsided: one bar towers over all twelve others. Broad Street: 359 deaths. The next-largest pump claims just 64.

What does this bar chart show?

Each bar counts deaths at a given distance from the Broad Street pump. Hunt for a trend — honestly.
Step 8 of 9

Is the pump really the cause?

A cluster near a pump could be a coincidence — lots of people simply live there. So test the idea directly: if the Broad Street pump is the source, deaths should fall off as you walk away from it.

They do, sharply. Deaths peak one to three blocks out and collapse beyond four. A clear relationship between distance and deaths is real evidence — unlike a flat, trendless chart, this one points straight at the pump.

Step 9 of 9

Name the finding: the Broad Street pump

The cluster centers on the Broad Street pump: 359 of 578 deaths are nearest to it, and deaths fall off with every block away. Snow took this map to the parish officials and asked them to remove the pump's handle so no one could draw water. The outbreak was already fading — but the map changed how the world understands disease.

Case closed

You found the Broad Street pump.

You started from one death at one location and built a full evidence chain — the same moves John Snow used to invent epidemiology, and the same moves a working data scientist makes today.

  • A single death became a dataset of 578 real records.
  • The deaths weren't random — they formed a dense cluster, not an even sprinkle.
  • Every record carries location, date, and household details.
  • Overlaying the 13 pumps revealed one at the cluster's heart: Broad Street.
  • A bar chart showed Broad Street with 359 deaths — six times its nearest rival.
  • Deaths fell off sharply with distance from the pump — a real relationship, not a flat line.
  • The finding has a name and a fix: the Broad Street pump — remove the handle.
