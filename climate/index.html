The Breathing Planet

Some data only makes sense when you watch it over time. In 1958 Charles David Keeling started measuring the amount of carbon dioxide in the air at a remote observatory on Mauna Loa. Month after month, year after year, the readings piled up into a line with a story. In this lesson you'll read that line the way a scientist does — separating the trend from the cycle from the noise.

Step 3 of 9

The reveal

Here is the whole record. If the air's CO₂ just jittered up and down by chance, the line would wander sideways and go nowhere.

It doesn't. It climbs — relentlessly — from about 316 to over 430 ppm, while a small yearly wiggle rides on top. Press Shuffle time to scramble the readings out of order: the climb vanishes into noise. Order in time is the whole story.

Step 6 of 9

Decompose: trend plus cycle

One of the most powerful moves in time-series analysis is decomposition — splitting a messy line into simpler signals. This curve is really two things added together: a smooth long-term trend, and a repeating seasonal cycle.

The cycle is the planet breathing: as Northern-Hemisphere forests leaf out each spring and summer, they pull CO₂ from the air (a dip), then release it in fall and winter (a rise) — about 7 ppm up and down every year.

Step 7 of 9

The rate lens

Leave the raw line for a moment. For each decade, measure how fast CO₂ rose — its parts per million per year — and stack those rates into a bar chart. Grouping by a variable (here, the decade) is one of the most powerful moves in analysis.

The bars don't hold steady: they grow. CO₂ climbed about 0.9 ppm/year in the 1960s — and about 2.5 ppm/year in the 2020s. The rise itself is speeding up.

Step 8 of 9

Trend, and a warning about straight lines

Is there a trend? Unmistakably — a strong upward one. But here's the subtle move a careful analyst makes: draw a straight-line fit through the whole record and watch what it gets wrong.

The straight line can't bend, so it drifts off the data — and at the recent end the real curve pulls upward and away from the ruler. The true trend isn't linear; it curves upward, because the rise is accelerating. Assuming "steady and straight" would under-predict today's CO₂.

Step 9 of 9

Name the finding: the Keeling Curve

The line you read is the Keeling Curve. In this real record, CO₂ has risen about 117 ppm since 1958 — crossing 350 in 1987, 400 in 2015, and 420 in 2023 — while breathing in a steady yearly cycle and climbing ever faster. It is the clearest single picture of a changing atmosphere.
