Are We Alone?

For all of history, "are there other worlds like ours?" was a question for philosophers. Since 1995 it has become a question for data. Astronomers have now confirmed thousands of planets around other stars. In this lesson you'll plot them with their real measurements — size and starlight — and discover for yourself how many could be rocky, temperate worlds like Earth.

Find the labeled dot. That is 51 Pegasi b — the first planet found around a sun-like star.
Step 1 of 9

One world, 50 light-years away

In 1995, Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz detected 51 Pegasi b — the first confirmed planet circling a star like our Sun. It turned out to be a "hot Jupiter": a giant world whipping around its star in just four days, far too scorched for life.

On a chart, it's one point with a size and a temperature. What makes a single planet worth investigating?

What a planet knows

"We never store just a dot. Each confirmed planet carries a radius and often a mass, an orbit (its period and distance from its star), the starlight it receives, and the method and year we found it. The chart draws two of those numbers — the rest sit underneath."

Every value is a real NASA Archive measurement. Fields left blank were simply never measured for that planet — so we don't invent them.

Step 6 of 9

Filter to the worlds that matter

"Earth-like" has a testable meaning. A planet is a candidate for life if it is roughly rocky-sized (about 0.5–1.6 Earth radii) and gets roughly Earth-like starlight (0.25–1.5 times Earth's) — not so much that oceans boil, not so little that they freeze. That's the green box.

Turn on the box and lower the size filter. The thousands collapse to a small group sitting near Earth's own spot.

Step 7 of 9

The distribution lens

Leave the scatter for a moment. Count how many planets fall in each size range and stack them into a histogram. Grouping by a variable — here, radius — is one of the most powerful moves in analysis.

The bars tower in the "super-Earth to Neptune" range. Earth-sized worlds (near 1 R⊕) are a minority, not the norm — at least among the planets we've managed to find.

Step 8 of 9

Why does the catalog look like this?

Here's the twist a good data scientist always checks: a pattern can come from the world or from how we measured it. Color each planet by its discovery method and a bias appears — our main techniques find big planets close to their stars most easily, because those block more light and tug their stars harder.

So the empty "Earth-like" corner is partly real and partly a blind spot: small, temperate worlds are simply the hardest to detect. Reading data means asking how it was collected.

Step 9 of 9

Name the finding: rare, but not zero

Out of thousands of confirmed worlds, only a small group sits in the Earth-like box — rocky-sized and bathed in Earth-like starlight. They are humanity's first real shortlist of places that could host liquid water, and the next great telescopes are aimed straight at them.
