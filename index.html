Map Earth's Anger

At 5:46 a.m. on January 17, 1995, the ground beneath Kobe, Japan ripped apart. In this lesson you'll start from that single point on a map and build the evidence — coordinates, geometric patterns, and statistical graphs — to explain where earthquakes happen and why. Every earthquake dot is real: a random sample of 800 M5.5+ events (2000–2025) from the USGS earthquake catalog, plus the 1995 Kobe mainshock itself.

Find the labeled dot. That is Kobe — 135.19°E, 34.69°N.

The reveal

Here is the whole sample on a flat world map — 800 real earthquakes from 25 years of records. If earthquakes struck at random, the dots would sprinkle evenly across the whole map — oceans, continents, everywhere.

They don't. The dots gather into lines, arcs, and clusters, tracing the edges of the Pacific. Geometry vocabulary suddenly becomes data vocabulary.

Filter to sharpen the pattern

Step 7 of 9

The distribution lens

Leave the map for a moment. Count how many quakes fall in each magnitude bin and stack them into a histogram. The shape that appears has a name: a long right tail. Small quakes are everyday events; giants are vanishingly rare — but they exist, far out on the right.

Step 8 of 9

Are deeper earthquakes stronger?

It sounds plausible: deeper rupture, bigger quake. A scatter plot lets the data answer. If depth drove magnitude, the cloud of dots would climb as you read rightward.

Look carefully. In this real USGS sample the cloud stays flat — shallow, intermediate, and deep quakes all span the same range of magnitudes. Finding no clear relationship is a real finding, and an important one.

Step 9 of 9

Name the finding: the Pacific Ring of Fire

The band your dots traced is the Pacific Ring of Fire — the chain of boundary zones wrapping the Pacific Ocean. In this USGS sample, most M6+ dots cluster along this path, and Kobe sits on its western edge.
